The Christmas Truce of 1914: Beer, Schnapps and Plum Pudding

History · 2026-11-28

Photo: Niko Nieminen on Unsplash

On Christmas 1914, British and German soldiers left their trenches to swap cigars, schnapps, beer and plum pudding. Here is what they really drank, and what is legend.

Of all the drinks ever shared at Christmas, few carry as much weight as the ones passed across no man's land in December 1914. Barely five months into the First World War, on a frozen stretch of the Western Front in Flanders and northern France, thousands of British and German soldiers stopped fighting, climbed out of their trenches and met in the middle to wish each other a happy Christmas.

It began on Christmas Eve. British sentries noticed lanterns and small fir trees appearing along the German parapets, then heard singing drift over the wire. Stille Nacht was answered by English carols, and shouted greetings soon followed. The Imperial War Museums record that by Christmas morning men from both sides were meeting face to face, often after the Germans, many of them Saxons, made the first move.

What did they share? Mostly whatever was in their pockets. Contemporary letters and diaries describe tobacco, cigarettes, chocolate, sausages, buttons and badges changing hands, along with plenty of drink. German soldiers handed over schnapps and cigars; a Scots Guards private noted in his diary that he was given a glass of whisky; British tins of bully beef and slices of plum pudding went the other way. Many British troops had just received Princess Mary's brass gift tin, and its cigarettes and tobacco became instant currency.

Beer has its own famous episode. Frank Richards of the 2nd Royal Welch Fusiliers, near Houplines, later wrote that the Germans rolled barrels over from a local brewery, and he grumbled that the French beer was rather poor stuff. Captain Stockwell of the same battalion reportedly swapped a plum pudding for a barrel, and on Boxing Day morning he and his German counterpart saluted one another before shots were fired into the air to mark the end of their truce.

Then there is football. Several regimental histories and letters mention kickabouts with tins or sandbags for balls, and a German account claims a 3-2 win over the Scots. Historians at The Long, Long Trail stress that the football is the hardest part to pin down, and that later memoirs and newspaper stories added a layer of legend. The truce was also patchy: in some sectors firing never stopped, and men were killed on Christmas Day.

Estimates suggest around 100,000 men took part across a large part of the British line. Senior commanders on both sides were furious, orders against fraternising followed, and no comparable truce happened in 1915. By New Year 1915 the guns had resumed almost everywhere.

This festive season, raise a glass with that winter in mind. A rich, nutty tawny port or a glass of German Riesling beside your Christmas pudding makes a fitting toast to the men who, for one night, chose a shared drink over the rifle.
