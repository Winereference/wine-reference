How to become a sommelier with no experience: a step-by-step guide

Roles · 2026-11-16

No certificate or wine job yet? Here is the realistic route into sommelier work: the first restaurant job, which entry courses to take, what they cost and how to get noticed.

I get asked this question more than any other: can you become a sommelier if you have never worked with wine? The short answer is yes. There is no legal licence or compulsory qualification for the job in the UK, and plenty of the best sommeliers I have worked alongside started as waiters, bartenders, chefs or in a completely different career. What you need is curiosity, stamina, a genuine liking for looking after people, and a plan for turning that into knowledge and experience.

First, understand what the job really is. A sommelier is not just the person who opens the bottle. Depending on the restaurant, the role mixes hospitality, selling, building and costing the list, receiving and storing stock, training colleagues and talking to suppliers. The Wine & Spirit Education Trust makes the same point in its own advice on the career: attitude and care for guests matter at least as much as technical knowledge, because tasting and theory can be taught but warmth cannot.

Step one is to get inside a restaurant, ideally one that takes wine seriously. A runner, waiter or bar role in a place with a proper list and an experienced sommelier team is worth more than any course on its own. Be reliable, learn the floor, and let the wine team know you are interested. Offer to help polish glasses, carry cases into the cellar, count stock or set up for tastings. Beverage directors interviewed by SevenFifty Daily repeatedly say that they promote people who show dependability and teamwork on the floor first, and that guest care beats encyclopaedic knowledge.

Step two is structured study. For complete beginners I usually suggest the WSET Level 2 Award in Wines as a first course. It has no entry requirements, covers the main grapes and regions plus how to describe wine, and ends with a one-hour, closed-book paper of 50 multiple-choice questions. WSET states about 28 hours of total study, made up of 16 hours of tuition, 11 hours of private study and the exam. At the WSET School in London the in-person course is listed at 630 pounds and the online version at 465 pounds; prices at other approved providers vary, so shop around and ask whether your employer will contribute.

If you already have some hospitality experience and want a service-focused credential, look at the Court of Master Sommeliers Europe Introductory Sommelier Certificate. It runs as two days of teaching with the exam on the morning of the third, covering viticulture and winemaking, the main wine regions, spirits, service technique, food matching and an introduction to tasting. You sit a multiple-choice theory paper and a short practical service test, and need 60 per cent in each. The Court itself says it is not a beginner wine course and strongly recommends service experience first. Its UK price list for 2025 shows 695 pounds for the Introductory course and exam, with the Certified exam at 325 pounds; check the current figure before booking, as fees change.

Step three is tasting with purpose. Reading alone will not make you a sommelier. Join or start a small tasting group with colleagues, share bottles, and practise describing what is in the glass out loud using a consistent method. Go to the free trade tastings that importers, merchants and generic wine bodies hold in London and other cities: they are the cheapest way to taste widely and the best way to meet people. WSET also recommends reading good trade and consumer titles, entering competitions and, if you can, working a harvest to see how wine is actually made.

Step four is finding mentors and making the move. Ask the head sommelier where you work whether you can shadow a service, sit in on a supplier meeting or help with the monthly stock take. Outside your own restaurant, a short, polite message asking a respected sommelier for twenty minutes of advice works more often than you would think. When a commis or assistant sommelier role comes up, research the restaurant thoroughly before the interview: know the chef, the style of food and a few wines on the list. Hiring managers quoted by SevenFifty Daily say candidates have lost offers over not knowing the basics about the place.

Be realistic about the pace. Most people spend somewhere between several months and a couple of years on the floor before their first sommelier title, often as a commis sommelier carrying glasses, decanting and running bottles while the senior team takes the orders. That apprenticeship is where you learn timing, temperature, how to handle a corked bottle without fuss and how to read a table. Pay at entry level is modest and varies widely by city and venue, so treat service charge, staff meals and paid training as part of the package when comparing offers.

My last piece of advice is simple: stay humble and keep going. You will mispronounce producers, open the wrong vintage and get a blind tasting completely wrong in front of colleagues. Everyone does. The sommeliers who progress are the ones who learn from those moments, keep tasting, and never forget that the guest's evening matters more than showing off what they know.

Key points

  • No qualification is legally required to work as a sommelier in the UK; experience and attitude get you hired.
  • Start in any front-of-house role at a restaurant with a serious wine list and make your interest known to the wine team.
  • WSET Level 2 Award in Wines is a sensible first course: no prerequisites, about 28 study hours and a 50-question multiple-choice exam.
  • The CMS Europe Introductory Sommelier Certificate adds service skills but expects some hospitality experience first.
  • Check current course fees before booking: WSET School London and CMS Europe publish their prices, and other providers vary.
  • Taste weekly with a group and attend free trade tastings to build your palate and your contacts.
  • Find a mentor, ask to shadow service, and research every restaurant thoroughly before an interview.
