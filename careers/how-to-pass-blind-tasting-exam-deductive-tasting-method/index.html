How to pass the blind tasting exam: the deductive tasting method explained

Tasting · 2026-11-23

A head sommelier's practical guide to the deductive tasting grid: what the Court of Master Sommeliers tasting exams ask of you, how to practise and how to reach a confident conclusion.

Of all the parts of a sommelier exam, the blind tasting is the one that keeps candidates awake at night. I remember the feeling well: a row of anonymous glasses, a clock running and the nagging fear that your palate will simply go blank. The good news is that blind tasting is far less about magic and far more about method. Once you understand how the deductive grid works and practise it properly, the exam becomes a structured conversation with the wine rather than a guessing game.

Start by knowing exactly what you will face. At the Court of Master Sommeliers Certified Sommelier exam, the tasting section currently gives you four wines, two whites and two reds, and 45 minutes in which to complete them. According to the Court's own candidate guide, you receive a paper grid for each wine to jot notes on, but your answers go into exam software on your own laptop, and the paper submits itself automatically when time runs out. That works out at roughly eleven minutes a wine, and the Court suggests having the whites finished by half time. At the Advanced level the format changes completely: six wines in 25 minutes, spoken aloud to a panel of Master Sommeliers who mark only what you say. Always check the latest candidate documents before you book, as formats are revised.

The grid itself is your best friend. The current Certified grid walks you through sight, nose and palate before conclusions. You note the colour, then decide which families of fruit are present, from apple, pear, citrus, stone and tropical fruit in whites to red, black and blue fruit in reds, naming one specific example for each yes. You then judge the condition of that fruit, whether tart, ripe, jammy, baked or dried, and work through a list of non-fruit markers such as flowers, herbs, pyrazine-style green notes, lees, malolactic character, earth, minerality and new oak. Finally you assess structure: sweetness and bitterness for whites, tannin for reds, plus acidity and alcohol on a simple scale.

Read the instructions on scoring carefully, because they change how you should behave. The Court states that there are no deductions and that each line carries marks, so you must actively tick no when something is absent rather than leaving the box blank. It also advises keeping descriptors short and only answering yes when a character is obvious and helps identify the wine. In other words, an empty line is lost marks, and a vague or excessive list of aromas wastes precious seconds. Precision beats poetry.

The conclusion is where the deduction comes together. On the Certified grid you choose a grape from the examinable list, decide whether the climate was cool, moderate or warm, name the country, give an age range and write a sales pitch of no more than two sentences, with space to explain why you rejected the alternatives. My advice, echoed in a detailed GuildSomm preparation guide by Martin Beally, is to settle the grape variety first and only then work out origin. Look for several objective clues, things you can actually measure such as acid level, tannin, alcohol, oak or botrytis, and give the most weight to those that point to the variety itself. When a clue is loud, trust it rather than hedging.

How should you practise? Tasting lots of different wines is useful, but the biggest gains come from side-by-side comparisons of wines that are easily confused. The Court's own guidance suggests pairs like Chablis and Sancerre, and the GuildSomm guide recommends spending the large majority of your practice time on comparative flights rather than full six-wine mock exams. Taste wines both blind and sighted, so you learn what a textbook example of each grape smells like as well as how to spot it. Keep a written log of every blind attempt; over a few months it will show you exactly which grapes you confuse and where your calibration drifts.

Calibration deserves its own session. Many candidates misjudge alcohol and acidity because they have never compared, for example, a 12.5 per cent and a 14.5 per cent wine side by side. Set up small structural flights with your tasting group, revealing the label afterwards, until your medium plus and high are consistent. Do the same for tannin in reds and for phenolic grip in whites. A tasting group of three to five committed colleagues who meet weekly, take turns to bring wines and mark each other honestly is, in my experience, the single most effective study tool there is.

Treat the clock as part of the exam. Practise with a timer from the start, aiming to finish each Certified-style wine in under eleven minutes, and rehearse the verbal format aloud if you are heading for Advanced. The Court notes that at Advanced level the clock starts as soon as you touch a glass and that you must commit to a single answer for each structural element rather than a range. Learn your grid so thoroughly that you never need to think about the order, and your mind will be free to focus on the wine.

Finally, remember why the method exists. If you study with WSET, its Systematic Approach to Tasting builds the same habit of describing wine in a consistent, structured way, step by step through the levels. Whichever path you follow, deductive tasting is ultimately a service skill: it teaches you to describe a wine quickly and accurately for a guest, to spot faults and to recommend with confidence. Pass or not on the day, every hour you put into it will make you a better sommelier on the floor.

Key points

  • Read the latest candidate guide: the Certified exam currently has four wines (two white, two red) in 45 minutes; Advanced has six wines in 25 minutes, delivered verbally.
  • Learn the tasting grid until the order is automatic: sight, nose, palate, structure, then conclusions.
  • Fill every line and tick no when a character is absent; on the Certified exam there are no deductions.
  • Decide the grape first, then origin, using several objective clues such as acid, alcohol, tannin and oak.
  • Spend most practice time on side-by-side comparisons of easily confused wines, like Chablis and Sancerre.
  • Calibrate acidity, alcohol and tannin with structured flights and reveal the labels afterwards.
  • Keep a log of every blind tasting to find your blind spots, and always practise against a timer.
